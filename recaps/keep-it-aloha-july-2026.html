Keep It Aloha Bets on the Next Generation

Saturday July 4 – Sunday July 5, 2026
Sat & Sun 10 AM–6 PM
SALT at Our Kaka’ako, Honolulu
Free · all ages
~3,000
Collectors · 2 Days
Free
All Ages

Keep It Aloha was back at SALT at Our Kaka’ako for the July 4th weekend — the third edition of the free, all-ages pau hana card show from @kamakarips and @hicollectibles. Somewhere around 3,000 collectors came through over the two days.

But ask the organizers and the turnout isn’t the story. This edition made clear what Keep It Aloha is really building: not just a card show, but a community — and a place for the next generation.

The Keiki Corner

The heart of the weekend was the Keiki Corner: free table space set aside for kids to sell and trade their own cards, many of them for the very first time. Two young vendors stood out — a 12-year-old who sold a couple hundred dollars of cards and lit up (he’s already planning to be at the next one), and a 17-year-old on day two who showed up last-minute, sat down, and just started dealing.

The idea, in the organizers’ words, is to cultivate the next generation of entrepreneurs. Running a table teaches negotiation, pricing, and reading value — skills that are genuinely hard to break into, for kids and adults alike. It’s the same thread that runs through their “From Cards to Capitol” ethos. The corner was sponsored by Collectr (with Plinko giveaways), and by the end of the weekend a bunch of parents had signed their kids up.

A Cameo from David Choe

One of the weekend’s biggest surprises: artist David Choe stopped by. If the name’s new to you, Choe is the artist famous for taking early Facebook stock instead of cash for a mural — a bet that reportedly made him a multimillionaire — and he’s since turned up on Joe Rogan’s podcast and in the series Beef. He also has his own sought-after line of packs, and he brought some to the floor and did a few deals with local vendors.

Then he did the most on-brand thing possible for this show: he headed over to the Keiki Corner and handed cards to the kids. A world-famous artist giving cards to keiki at their first vending table — that’s exactly the kind of moment Keep It Aloha is built to make.

Built for families: this may have been the biggest keiki turnout of any card show in Hawaii — and not just kids being shepherded around by parents. Balloon animals, plenty to keep little ones busy, and a genuinely family-friendly floor. The organizers watched people who don’t even collect Pokémon buy their first packs and rip them open with their kids — a way to connect, and a quiet first lesson in how the hobby (and holding something as it grows in value) works. Future plans lean further in: carnival games out front, so a parent who knows nothing about cards can still do something with their family.

Taking Care of the Vendors

Vendor hospitality is a point of pride here. Table prices were kept low on purpose so first-time vendors could give it a shot — and plenty did. Moko Banana Bread donated over 60 loaves for the vendors, and water came from Waiakea.

One fun wrinkle: some vendors offered a “rip price” — a discount if you opened your packs right there at the table — which turned pack-opening into a shared, communal moment rather than a solo gamble at home. Little touches like that are the whole point.

Community First

None of this is an accident. Keep It Aloha started as a free show — the organizers openly lost money on the first one — because they simply felt the Pokémon community here was worth gathering. Three communities have grown up around it now: vendors, collectors, and, increasingly, families. The founding principle, as they put it plainly, was community first — Ohana, no judgment, everyone’s story welcome.

It’s become good for the whole neighborhood, too: SALT has given Keep It Aloha the first weekend of every month through the end of the year, and all those collectors spill out into Kaka’ako afterward for food, drinks, and — by the organizers’ count — a whole lot of ice cream.
